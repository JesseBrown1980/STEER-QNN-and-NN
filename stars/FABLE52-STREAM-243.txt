1. Introduction
1.1 Background
Large language models are trained to predict the next token in a sequence. Given a context
x_1, ..., x_{t-1}, the model assigns a probability p(x_t | x_1, ..., x_{t-1}) to each token
in its vocabulary. The parameters are fitted by minimising the cross-entropy between the
predicted distribution and the observed text. In this paper we ask a different question:
what can be learned about such a model from a short sample of its output alone?
1.2 Contributions
We make three contributions. First, we show that a table of 4,096 columns built from a few
kilobytes of emission predicts the next symbol of unrelated text more often than chance.
Second, we show that the agreement between two such tables built from different models
marks the steps at which either is right. Third, we find that the effect does not survive
when the table is scrambled, which rules out the trivial explanation.
2. Related work
See [1], [2, 3] and the references therein. Our setting is closest to that of [4], but we
use no gradient and no floating point arithmetic.
3. Method
Let b_1, ..., b_n be the bytes of the emission and q_i = floor(b_i / 4). We count triples
(q_{3j+1}, q_{3j+2}, q_{3j+3}) in a cube of 64 x 64 x 64 cells and define the emission of
column (u, v) as the w maximising the count, with ties broken toward the smaller w.
4. Results
Table 1 shows the hit rate. Figure 2 shows the growth. All differences are in the predicted
direction on the first text and in the same direction, but smaller, on the second.
5. Limitations
One seat, one emission, two texts of one megabyte. We did not separate the two effects.
